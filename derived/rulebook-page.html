A card game of the Deccan · 2–8 players · about 20 minutes

DECCAN

Two armies form on one field. You do not command either of them — you put your units into one, alongside rivals doing the same, and you are paid for what you kill.

72 cards 8 rulers × 9 units Seven kills wins

Value is agility, inverted

Every unit has one number. It is what the unit deals, what it can survive, and — read backwards — when it acts.

A charge resolves band by band, cheapest first. A unit killed before its band comes up never strikes. So three Horsemen can take an Elephant off the field before it swings once, and the Elephant — the most expensive card in the box — is the one that acts last.

The words that carry rules

Value
The number on a unit. What it deals, what it survives, and inverted, when it acts.
Band
Every unit of the same value. A band strikes together, at one instant.
The charge
The battle. Both armies turn face up and resolve at once.
The line
An army is a line, not a pile. Units are laid left to right in the order they were committed, and never reordered. The leftmost card has been standing longest.
Leftmost wins
The one tie-breaker in the game. Whenever two units are equally eligible for anything — taking a blow, taking the credit for a kill, being named by a Spy — the leftmost is chosen. You never need to remember anything: look at the table.
Stand
A unit that survives a charge stays on the field, face up, undamaged.
Recover
A unit that leaves the field returns to its owner's hand and sits out one full lap of the table.
Senior partner
The player holding the most value standing in an army. Only they may call the charge.

Eight units: five bodies, and three that do something. Three verbs, and they do not overlap — the Commander hits, the Spy removes, the Scout looks.

The Commander

It copies the blow, not the body. Its durability stays 1 — anything kills it. It copies the enemy's best, not its own army's, and Commanders do not copy Commanders, or two facing each other would define one another. Facing nothing but Commanders it strikes for 1.

Because it reads the field after the Spies have fired, a Spy that pulls out the enemy Elephant also shrinks the enemy Commander's blow.

The Spy

It names the highest-value enemy unit. That unit goes home and recovers. It does not strike, it cannot be killed, and nobody scores a point for it. Two Spies may not name the same unit. The Spy is not spent doing this — it stays and fights on.

Spies facing each other do not cancel out. Each names the highest enemy unit, which is almost never the other Spy — so both Elephants walk home and the two Spies are left standing in an empty field, killing each other for a point apiece.

The Scout

It goes out face up, so the whole table knows you scouted. What you saw is yours — the card you looked at stays face down for everyone else. Then it stands there, where everybody can see that you know something they do not.

Decided, not yet built

The model still deploys the Scout face down and gives it a public reveal — the card turns face up for the whole table — rather than a private look. Those are different cards: a reveal buys something for everyone and is paid for by one person. Nothing about the Scout should be quoted from a measurement until this is fixed.

Setup

Each player takes a ruler — a fixed deck of nine units, held in hand.

Put out two army areas, each with room for a line of four. An army holds at most four units, from at most four players.

Youngest player begins; play passes to the left.

A turn

Take exactly one action.

DEPLOY

Put one unit from your hand face down into an army, at the right-hand end of the line. (A Scout deploys face up.) You may only ever stand in one army — once you have a unit in one, every later unit joins it.

WITHDRAW

Take one of your units off the field. It recovers. This is what un-jams a full line, and it costs a turn and nothing else.

DEFECT

Move your whole contingent to the other army. Only legal when a single unit is all you have standing — so crossing takes preparation: withdraw down to one, sit through its recovery, then go. An ally thinning out is visibly getting ready to leave.

CALL THE CHARGE

Only the senior partner of an army, and only when both armies hold at least one unit. You cannot charge at nobody.

PASS

Do nothing.

If both armies are full, the charge happens immediately, whether anyone wants it or not — that is what stops the game dying of hesitation. If the table passes round and round with nobody willing to act, the game ends where it stands.

The charge

  1. Reveal. Every face-down unit in both armies turns face up.
  2. The Spies fire. Each Spy names the highest-value enemy unit still unnamed. Those units leave the field and recover. They never fought, and no one is paid for them.
  3. The bands resolve, lowest value first. Everything printed 1 strikes, then 2, and so on up to 6. Within a band, everything strikes at the same instant; after a band resolves, anything that has taken damage equal to its value is dead.

    A unit killed in an earlier band never strikes.

    Each striker puts its blow into the enemy unit it can finish; if it can finish none, into the biggest one still standing. Ties go leftmost. Damage stacks across bands — two Warriors together finish a Cannon neither could finish alone.

    A big unit usually never gets to choose. A Cannon 5 facing a Horseman 2 and a Warrior 3 takes 2 and then 3 and is dead at band 3, before band 5 comes round.

  4. Count the kills. For each unit that died, work out who takes the credit — one point, to one player, and it cannot be split.
    a. The single largest blow takes it. A Rifleman 4 at the back of the line beats a Horseman 2 at the front, because 4 is bigger than 2.
    b. If the largest blows tie, the leftmost takes it — the unit that has been standing in the line longest.
  5. The Scouts look.
  6. The field — but only if the charge drew blood. If nothing died, nobody takes the field. Otherwise the army with more surviving value takes it. It scores nothing at all — the field is worth only the turn that comes with it. If the values tie, nobody takes it.

    Why the blood condition exists: after a charge the front is quiet by construction, so a second charge straight away kills nothing. Without this the caller's side would still take the field on surviving value, hand the turn back to its own senior partner, and they could do it again — and again, for ever.

  7. Sort out the survivors. The dead go home and recover. Survivors stand, face up, whole — damage does not carry between charges. They keep their place in the line; gaps close without reshuffling, and anything committed afterwards goes to their right, so a veteran outranks every fresh card.
  8. Work out seniority again, then play on. With the dead cleared, each army's senior partner is whoever now holds the most value standing in it. The senior partner of the army that took the field takes the next turn; if nobody took the field, play continues in order.
    The starter is often not the caller

    Seniority is recounted on the survivors, so calling a charge that kills your own biggest unit hands the next turn — and the army — to somebody else. Over 14,664 charges, seniority changed hands in 41% of the charges that had a winner.

First to commit, not first to strike

Units of the same value are the same band and strike at the same instant — there is no first blow and no last blow to point at. The tie is broken by something that happened turns earlier, when the card was put down.

That is the whole reason an army is laid out as a line. Who committed first is not something anyone has to remember: it is the card on the left.

Three players each commit a Horseman against an Elephant. 2 + 2 + 2 is exactly 6, so all three blows are needed, and all three tie:

A — leftmostspent a Horseman → 1 point
Bspent a Horseman → nothing
Cspent a Horseman → nothing

You fight together and you are scored separately. Somebody has to commit first, everyone can see who has not, and the front of the line is worth having.

Scoring

One point for every enemy unit your units killed. Seven points wins.

Nothing else scores. Not holding the field, not surviving, not the size of what you killed — an Elephant and a Scout are each worth exactly one point.

A charge can carry two players past seven at once, and they share the win. Not a freak result — over 9,000 games it happens 11.5% of the time, because a single charge pays several players and the seventh point is often not the last one scored. If your table wants a single winner, break it on who holds the field.

Seniority

The first player to put a unit into an empty army is its senior partner. After that it is whoever holds the most value standing in it; on a tie the incumbent keeps it. Lose everything and the army has no senior partner until someone deploys.

The senior partner chooses when the charge happens — for everybody, including the three players whose units are standing in the line beside theirs.

Why it is built this way

Everything recovers. Nothing is destroyed. A killed unit goes home, waits a lap, and comes back. The cost of losing is tempo, not material — which is what lets a twenty-minute game contain seven kills and still have every deck intact at the end.

After a charge the front is quiet by construction. Every survivor stands facing units it could not finish, or it would have. Nothing more dies until somebody commits a fresh card — so every deployment is a deliberate act of violence, and withdrawing is a real move rather than a concession.

Timing is the game. Charge now against a thin line for a small harvest, or wait while both sides thicken and the killing gets bigger — knowing the enemy is arming too, and that whoever calls it chooses the moment for everyone.

Your army is not your team. Kills are scored by the player who landed the blow, so the four of you in one line are competing for the same corpses.

What is not settled

The roster. The eight real rulers have not been searched against these cards. This is the largest open item in the game.
The Scout. Decided as a private look; the model still implements a public reveal.
What an ability costs. The roster search prices Spy, Scout and Commander at their printed 1 — one less than the cheapest fighter — so an ability is a discount, and the box has to be stocked by a quota rather than by the card being worth taking.
Two players. Seven kills heads-up runs longer per person than at any other count.
Whether anyone will ever gang up. Three Horsemen are needed to kill an Elephant and only one of the three is paid. That is either the central tension of a game where you fight together and score apart, or the reason cheap units never combine and the band ladder never fires at a table. A simulation cannot answer it — bots do not mind being used. Play it.
